Spacing Effect
The tendency for information to be better remembered when study sessions are spaced out over time rather than crammed into a single session.
Specific Phobia
An anxiety disorder characterized by an intense, irrational fear of a specific object or situation that leads to avoidance and significant distress.
Spermarche
The first ejaculation of sperm in males, marking the onset of sexual maturity and reproductive capability during puberty.
Split Brain Research
Studies of individuals whose corpus callosum has been severed to reduce severe epilepsy, showing how the left and right hemispheres of the brain can function independently and specialize in different tasks.
Split-Half Reliability
A measure of reliability in which a test is divided into two halves and scores on both halves are compared.
Spontaneous Recovery
The sudden reappearance of a previously extinguished conditioned response after a rest period, without further conditioning.
Spotlight Effect
The tendency to overestimate how much other people notice and pay attention to our appearance, actions, or mistakes.
Standard Deviation
A measure of how spread out data are from the mean (average). A low standard deviation means most scores are close to the mean, while a high standard deviation means the scores are more spread out.
Standardization
The process of administering and scoring a test in a consistent way for all test takers.
State Dependent Memory
The tendency to better recall information when a person is in the same internal state (mood, emotional state, or physiological condition) as when the information was learned.
Statistical Significance
A statistical statement of how likely it is that an obtained result occurred by chance (noted by p-value).
Stereotype
A generalized belief or expectation about a group of people that assumes members of the group share the same characteristics, often oversimplifying reality.
Stereotype Lift
Performance improvement that occurs when positive stereotypes about a group enhance confidence and outcomes.
Stereotype Threat
The risk of underperformance when individuals are aware of negative stereotypes about their group.
Stigma
A negative label or stereotype attached to individuals with psychological disorders that can lead to prejudice and discrimination.
Stimulants
Psychoactive drugs that increase activity in the central nervous system, leading to heightened alertness, energy, and arousal.
Stimulus Discrimination
The ability to distinguish between similar stimuli and respond only to the specific conditioned stimulus, not to other similar ones.
Stimulus Generalization
The tendency to respond to stimuli that are similar to the conditioned stimulus with the same conditioned response.
Storage
The stage of memory in which encoded information is held and preserved in the brain over time.
Stress
The physiological and psychological response to demands or challenges that threaten or exceed an individual’s ability to cope.
Stressors
Events, situations, or stimuli that trigger a physiological or psychological stress response.
Stroboscopic Motion
The illusion of continuous movement created when a series of slightly different still images are shown rapidly in succession, as in movies or animation.
Structural Encoding
The process of encoding information based on its physical appearance or visual features, such as shape, color, or format, rather than meaning or sound.
Subjective Well-Being
A person’s self-reported evaluation of their own happiness and life satisfaction, including the balance of positive and negative emotions.
Sublimation
A defense mechanism in which socially unacceptable impulses are redirected into socially acceptable or productive activities.
Substance P
A neurotransmitter involved in the transmission of pain messages to the brain.
Sunk-Cost Fallacy
The tendency to continue an endeavor because of previously invested time, money, or effort, even when future costs outweigh the benefits.
Superego
The part of personality that represents internalized moral standards and ideals, guiding behavior according to a sense of right and wrong.
Superordinate Goals
Shared goals that require cooperation between groups to achieve, often reducing conflict and increasing positive relationships among group members.
Supertasters
Individuals who have a higher-than-average number of taste buds, making them especially sensitive to certain tastes, particularly bitter flavors.
Survey
A technique for ascertaining the self-reported attitudes or behaviors of a particular group, usually by questioning a representative, random sample of the group.
Sympathetic Nervous System
The division of the autonomic nervous system that activates the body’s “fight-or-flight” response during stress or danger, increasing alertness and preparing the body for action.
Synesthesia
A perceptual phenomenon in which stimulation of one sensory or cognitive pathway leads to involuntary experiences in another, such as seeing colors when hearing music.
Syntax
The set of rules that governs the order and arrangement of words in a sentence to create meaningful and grammatically correct statements.
Systematic Desensitization
A behavioral therapy technique that combines gradual exposure to a feared stimulus with relaxation techniques to reduce anxiety and develop a new response to the fear.

Taijin Kyofusho
A culture-bound syndrome most commonly seen in Japan, characterized by an intense fear of offending or embarrassing others through one’s appearance, body odor, or behavior in social situations.
Tardive Dyskinesia
A movement disorder characterized by involuntary, repetitive muscle movements that can develop as a side effect of long-term use of antipsychotic medications.
Taste Aversion
A learned avoidance of a food or taste after a single experience of illness following its consumption, often occurring after just one pairing.
Telegraphic Speech
Early language use (around age 2) in which children speak in short, simple sentences that include only essential words, usually nouns and verbs (e.g., “want cookie” instead of “I want a cookie”).
Temperament
An individual’s inborn, biologically based patterns of emotional reactivity and self-regulation that appear early in life and influence how a person responds to their environment.
Temperance
A signature strength in positive psychology that involves self-control, moderation, and the ability to regulate impulses and behaviors.
Temporal Lobes
The part of the cerebral cortex involved in processing auditory information, language comprehension, and memory.
Tend and Befriend Theory
A theory suggesting that, in response to stress, people may cope by seeking social support and protecting or nurturing others rather than relying solely on the fight-or-flight response.
Teratogens
Harmful substances or environmental agents that can cause birth defects or developmental abnormalities during prenatal development, such as alcohol, certain drugs, or infections.
Test-Retest Reliability
The consistency of test scores when the same test is given to the same people at different times.
Testing Effect
The finding that actively testing oneself on material improves long-term memory more than simply re-studying the information.
Texture Gradient
A monocular depth cue in which coarse, detailed textures appear closer, while smoother, less detailed textures appear farther away.
Thalamus
A brain structure that acts as the brain’s main relay station, receiving sensory information and sending it to the appropriate areas of the cerebral cortex for processing.
Theory
An explanation using an integrated set of principles that organizes observations and predicts behaviors or events.
Theory of Mind
The ability to understand that other people have their own thoughts, beliefs, intentions, and perspectives that may differ from one’s own.
Therapeutic Alliance
The collaborative, trusting relationship between a therapist and client that supports effective treatment and positive therapeutic outcomes.
Theta Waves
Slow brain waves that are typically seen during light sleep (especially NREM Stage 1 and 2) and are also associated with deep relaxation and early stages of sleep.
Third Variable Problem
A situation in correlational research where an unmeasured, outside variable is responsible for the observed relationship between two other variables.
Tip-of-the-Tongue Phenomenon
The temporary inability to retrieve a known word or memory, accompanied by the feeling that retrieval is imminent.
Token Economies
An operant conditioning procedure in which people earn a token of some sort for exhibiting a desired behavior and can later exchange the tokens for various privileges or treats.
Tolerance
The diminishing effect with regular use of the same dose of a drug, requiring the user to take larger and larger doses to experience the effect.
Top-Down Processing
A type of perceptual processing that uses prior knowledge, expectations, and experience to interpret and make sense of sensory information.
Touch (Somatosensation)
The sense that allows the body to perceive pressure, temperature, pain, and body position through sensory receptors in the skin and other tissues.
Transcendence
A signature strength in positive psychology that involves appreciating beauty, finding meaning, and feeling a connection to something larger than oneself.
Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation
A biological treatment that uses magnetic pulses to stimulate specific areas of the brain, often to treat depression when other treatments have not been effective.
Transduction
The process by which sensory receptors convert physical energy from the environment (such as light, sound, or pressure) into neural signals that the brain can interpret.
Trichromatic Theory
The theory that color vision is based on three types of cones in the retina that are sensitive to red, green, and blue light, with all other colors created by combinations of these signals.
Trust and Mistrust
Erikson’s first psychosocial stage (infancy) in which infants develop a sense of safety and reliability in the world when caregivers are consistent and responsive; inconsistent care can lead to mistrust and insecurity.
Twin Studies
Research studies that compare identical and fraternal twins to examine the relative influence of genetics and environment on behavior and traits.
Two-Factor Theory
A theory of emotion that proposes that emotion is the result of physiological arousal and a cognitive label that interprets that arousal.

Unconditional Positive Regard
According to Carl Rogers, accepting and valuing a person without judgment or conditions, regardless of their behavior or feelings.
Unconditional Positive Regard
A therapist’s complete acceptance, empathy, and nonjudgmental support of a client, regardless of the client’s thoughts, feelings, or behaviors.
Unconditioned Response (UR)
An automatic, unlearned reaction to an unconditioned stimulus, such as salivating when food is presented.
Unconditioned Stimulus (UCS)
A stimulus that naturally and automatically triggers a response without prior learning, such as food causing salivation.
Unconscious
In Freud’s theory, the part of the mind that contains thoughts, memories, desires, and conflicts that are outside of conscious awareness but still influence behavior.
Upward Social Comparison
Comparing oneself to others who are more successful or better off, which can provide motivation for improvement but may also lower self-esteem.

Validity
The extent to which a test measures what it is intended to measure.
Variable
Anything that can vary and is feasible and ethical to measure.
Variable Interval
A reinforcement schedule in which a response is rewarded after unpredictable amounts of time have passed, leading to steady, moderate rates of responding.
Variable Ratio
A reinforcement schedule in which a response is rewarded after an unpredictable number of responses, producing high and steady rates of behavior (e.g., slot machines).
Vestibular Sense
The sensory system in the inner ear that helps maintain balance and posture by detecting changes in head movement and spatial orientation.
Vicarious Conditioning
Learning that occurs by observing the consequences (rewards or punishments) of another person’s behavior and adjusting one’s own behavior accordingly.
Visual Cliff
A laboratory setup used to test depth perception in infants and young animals, where a glass surface creates the illusion of a drop-off to measure responses to perceived height and danger.
Visuospatial Sketchpad
A component of working memory that temporarily stores and manipulates visual and spatial information, such as images, shapes, and location in space.
Volley Theory
The theory that groups of auditory neurons fire in rapid succession to help transmit sounds, especially for higher-frequency sounds that individual neurons cannot fire fast enough to encode alone.

Wavelength (vision)
The distance between successive peaks of a light wave, which determines the perceived color of light.
Weber’s Law
The principle that the just noticeable difference between two stimuli is a constant proportion of the original stimulus intensity, meaning larger stimuli require a greater change to detect a difference.
Wernicke’s Area
A region of the left temporal lobe involved in language comprehension; damage to this area can result in fluent but meaningless speech and difficulty understanding language.
Wisdom
A signature strength in positive psychology characterized by the ability to think critically, use knowledge effectively, and make sound judgments based on experience and understanding.
Withdrawal
The discomfort and distress that follow discontinuing an addictive drug or behavior.
Word Salad
A symptom of schizophrenia involving disorganized speech in which words and phrases are strung together incoherently, making communication unintelligible.
Working Memory
A limited-capacity memory system that temporarily holds and actively processes information needed for tasks such as learning, reasoning, and problem-solving.

A theory of arousal and performance that states performance improves with increased arousal up to an optimal point, after which further arousal leads to a decline in performance.
