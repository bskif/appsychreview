Unit 1: Biological Bases of Behavior

Topic 1.5: Sleep

Last Updated: June 23, 2026

The Big Picture: Mapping the Mind's Shifts

When you think of studying psychology, you might picture analyzing conscious choices, memories, or social behaviors. But a huge part of understanding our minds involves looking at what happens when we aren't awake at all. To truly grasp how our brains function, we have to start with the concept of consciousness and explore how our bodies cycle through different states of rest every single day.

Let's look at exactly how your brain manages the night shift.

1. The Levels of Awareness

Before jumping straight into sleep cycles, we need to talk about the overarching umbrella: consciousness.

  • Consciousness: A person’s awareness of themselves and their environment, including thoughts, feelings, sensations, and perceptions at any given moment. It isn't a simple on/off switch; it exists in varying levels throughout the day.
  • Sleep: A naturally recurring state of rest characterized by reduced consciousness, slowed bodily functions, and brain activity that supports restoration, memory consolidation, and overall health.

Our Internal Clock

Your body naturally tracks time using a system that aligns with the planet's rotation:

  • Circadian Rhythm: The body’s internal biological clock that regulates the sleep-wake cycle and other physiological processes on a roughly 24-hour schedule.

When our modern schedules or travel plans conflict with this built-in clock, we experience real physiological friction:

  • Jet Lag: Fatigue and sleep disturbance resulting from disruption of the body's normal circadian rhythm following long flights through different time zones. Your watch updates instantly, but your internal cellular timing takes days to catch up.
  • Shift Work: Work schedules that take place outside the traditional 9-to-5 day, often disrupting circadian rhythms and sleep patterns. Working regular overnights can throw off your internal rhythms and impact how alert you feel when you are awake.

2. Riding the Waves: The Stages of Sleep

When you drift off, your brain doesn't just turn off. Instead, it moves through a series of predictable stages, each identified by its own unique electroencephalogram (EEG) pattern.

Falling Asleep and Light Sleep (NREM)

Your journey into the night begins with non-REM (NREM) sleep stages, which actually decrease in duration as successive cycles repeat throughout the night.

  • Alpha Waves: Relatively slow brain waves that are most common when a person is awake but relaxed and calm, often occurring just before falling asleep.
  • NREM stage 1: A very light sleep stage that occurs as a transition from wakefulness to sleep, marked by slowed brain activity, muscle relaxation, and brief hypnagogic sensations.
  • Hypnogogic Sensations: Vivid, dreamlike experiences (such as falling or seeing flashes of light) that occur during the transition from wakefulness to sleep, especially in NREM Stage 1. This is what causes that sudden physical jerk that snaps you back awake!
  • Theta Waves: Slow brain waves that are typically seen during light sleep (especially NREM Stage 1 and 2) and are also associated with deep relaxation and early stages of sleep.
  • NREM stage 2: A deeper stage of light sleep characterized by slower heart rate and body temperature, with brain activity showing sleep spindles.
  • Sleep Spindles: Brief bursts of rapid brain activity that occur during NREM Stage 2 sleep and are believed to play a role in memory consolidation and protecting sleep from external disturbances.

Deep Sleep and the REM Paradox

  • NREM stage 3: The deepest stage of non-REM sleep, characterized by delta waves and important for physical restoration, growth, and recovery.
  • Delta Waves: Very slow, high-amplitude brain waves that occur primarily during deep NREM Stage 3 sleep.
  • REM Sleep: A stage of sleep marked by rapid eye movements, vivid dreaming, high brain activity, and temporary muscle paralysis.

REM sleep is considered paradoxical because its EEG printouts display active brain waves similar to wakefulness, yet the skeletal body is completely relaxed and immobilized. The frequency of REM sleep periods increases as your sleep cycle progresses through the night. If you miss out on it, your brain makes note of the debt:

  • REM Rebound: An increase in REM sleep that occurs after a person is deprived of REM sleep, causing the body to spend more time in REM during subsequent sleep cycles.

3. Why Do We Sleep and Dream?

Psychologists use distinct theoretical frameworks to explain why we spend a third of our lives in a state of reduced awareness. While scientists don't know exactly why we dream, we have some pretty good theories that help explain what is happening behind the scenes.

The Purpose of Sleep

  • Restoration (Sleep): The theory that sleep serves to repair and rejuvenate the body and brain, helping restore energy, support growth, and maintain overall physical and mental functioning.
  • Consolidation Theory: The theory that sleep, especially REM and deep NREM sleep, helps stabilize and strengthen newly formed memories so they can be stored long-term. Think of your brain as a file manager organizing your daily experiences while you rest.

The Structure of Dreams

  • Activation-Synthesis: The theory that dreams are the brain's attempt to make sense of random neural activity occurring during sleep. Under this model, your brainstem fires random signals during REM, and your higher brain regions synthesize those sparks into a story.

🔍 What’s on the Exam? According to the official exclusion statements in the CED, psychoanalytic dream theories (like Sigmund Freud's ideas on hidden meanings, latent content, and manifest content) are completely outside the scope of the exam. Keep your focus on biological and cognitive perspectives like activation-synthesis and consolidation!

4. Disruptions of the Night: Sleep Disorders

When healthy sleep patterns break down, it can seriously compromise physical health and daytime cognitive performance. The curriculum focuses on a specific set of clinical conditions:

  • Insomnia: A sleep disorder characterized by persistent difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or getting restful sleep despite adequate opportunity to do so.
  • Narcolepsy: A sleep disorder characterized by sudden, uncontrollable sleep attacks during waking hours, often including rapid entry into REM sleep and possible loss of muscle control.
  • Sleep Apnea: A sleep disorder characterized by temporary cessations of breathing during sleep and repeated momentary awakenings, often leaving individuals exhausted during the day.
  • REM Sleep Behavior Disorder: A sleep disorder in which the normal muscle paralysis of REM sleep is absent, causing a person to physically act out their dreams, sometimes violently.
  • Somnambulism: A sleep disorder in which a person walks or performs other complex behaviors while still asleep, usually during deep NREM Stage 3 sleep, with little or no memory of the event.
  • Night Terrors: A sleep disorder involving sudden episodes of intense fear, screaming, and high arousal during deep NREM sleep, typically with little to no recall of the event upon waking.

5. Don't Trip Up! (Common Misconceptions)

⚠️ Nightmares vs. Night Terrors: Students often mix these up on multiple-choice questions. Nightmares are vivid, scary dreams that occur during REM sleep, meaning you can usually remember them when you wake up. Night Terrors occur during deep NREM Stage 3 sleep, involve intense physical panic (like screaming or sweating), and leave almost no memory trace behind.

⚠️ Sleepwalking vs. REM Behavior Disorder: Since sleepwalkers are moving around, it is easy to assume they are acting out a dream. They aren't! Somnambulism happens during deep NREM Stage 3 sleep when the motor cortex is active but dreams aren't actively happening. Physically acting out a vivid dream because of a failure in protective muscle paralysis is specifically classified as REM Behavior Disorder.
