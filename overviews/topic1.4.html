Unit 1: Biological Bases of Behavior

Topic 1.4: The Brain

Last Updated: June 23, 2026

The Big Picture: Mapping the Master Control Center

Every single creative masterpiece ever painted, every scientific breakthrough ever calculated, and every subconscious heartbeat that keeps you alive originates from a three-pound mass of interconnected gray matter: the human brain. To understand human behavior, psychologists must first understand the localized biological machinery driving our minds.

To fully prepare for this essential section of the AP Psychology course, we divide our exploration of the brain into four structured segments:

  • The Subcortex and Deep Brain Structures: The evolutionary basement responsible for keeping you alive and alert.
  • The Limbic System and the Cerebral Cortex: The emotional processors and specialized lobes that build your complex consciousness.
  • Hemispheric Specialization and Split-Brain Research: How the left and right halves divide tasks and coordinate behaviors.
  • Plasticity and Tools of Discovery: The brain's incredible capacity to reshape itself and the advanced neuroimaging techniques we use to scan its inner workings.

Part 1: The Brainstem

We begin our journey at the base of the brain, exploring the oldest evolutionary regions. This area is often colloquially referred to as the "reptilian brain" because these deep structures are evolutionarily ancient and shared across vertebrate species, including reptiles. They do not manage complex thoughts, abstract philosophies, or deep cultural emotions; instead, they are dedicated entirely to primitive survival instincts, autonomic life-support functions, and fundamental motor coordination.

The Life-Support Blueprint

  • Brain Stem: The part of the brain that connects the brain to the spinal cord and controls basic life-sustaining functions such as breathing, heart rate, and blood pressure. If your upper brain goes offline, the brain stem can keep your body physically functioning.
  • Medulla: A part of the brainstem that controls vital involuntary functions such as breathing, heart rate, and blood pressure. Damage to the medulla is almost instantly fatal.
  • Pons: A part of the brainstem that helps regulate sleep, breathing, and arousal and acts as a bridge for communication between different parts of the brain. It coordinates movement signals passing between the motor cortex and the rest of the body.
  • Reticular Activating System: A network of neurons in the brainstem that regulates alertness, attention, and sleep-wake cycles by filtering incoming sensory information. It serves as the brain's internal volume knob; if it gets severely damaged, you can slip into a permanent coma.
  • Reward Center: A group of brain structures, including areas of the limbic system, that are activated by pleasurable experiences and reinforce behaviors through dopamine release. This pathway makes survival activities (like eating) feel rewarding, driving you to repeat them.
  • Cerebellum: A brain structure located at the back of the brain that coordinates balance, movement, and fine motor skills, and is also involved in motor learning. It is the home of your procedural memory, allowing you to walk, run, or play an instrument smoothly without conscious thought.

Part 2: The Limbic System and the Cerebral Cortex

Moving just above the subcortex (brainstem), we reach the specialized structures that manage emotional memory and complex thought processing.

The Limbic System: The Emotional Core

The Limbic System is a group of brain structures involved in emotion, motivation, and memory, including the hippocampus, amygdala, and parts of the hypothalamus.

  • Thalamus: A brain structure that acts as the brain’s main relay station, receiving sensory information and sending it to the appropriate areas of the cerebral cortex for processing. It routes all incoming senses except for smell directly to their correct cortical destinations.
  • Hypothalamus: A small brain structure that helps regulate essential body functions such as hunger, thirst, body temperature, and hormone release by controlling the endocrine system. It is the primary regulator of the "Four Fs": Fighting, Fleeing, Feeding, and Mating.
  • Pituitary Gland: A small gland often called the “master gland” because it releases hormones that control other endocrine glands and regulate growth, metabolism, and reproduction. It sits right under the hypothalamus, directly linking the nervous system to the endocrine system.
  • Hippocampus: A structure in the limbic system that is important for forming new memories and helping with learning and spatial navigation. It acts like a "save button" for explicit memories, processing them before they are stored long-term in the cortex.
  • Amygdala: A structure in the limbic system involved in processing emotions, especially fear and aggression, and in forming emotional memories. It functions as your internal threat detector.

The Cerebral Cortex: The Crown of Consciousness

Surrounding these deep structures is the Cerebral Cortex, the outer layer of the brain responsible for higher-level functions such as thinking, perceiving, language, memory, and voluntary movement(cortex is Latin for "bark" or "outer layer"). It is separated into two prominent Hemispheres (the left and right halves of the brain that specialize in different functions but communicate with each other through the corpus callosum).

The cortex is organized into four distinct anatomical regions, known as lobes, which repeat across both sides of the brain:

  • Frontal Lobes: The part of the cerebral cortex involved in decision-making, planning, reasoning, problem-solving, personality, and voluntary movement. It houses the Prefrontal Cortex (the forward part of the frontal lobes responsible for judgment, planning, and processing new memories), which handles Executive Functioning (cognitive processes that include working memory, flexible thinking, and self-control) and powers Higher-Order Thinking (complex cognitive processes such as analysis, synthesis, evaluation, and creation). At the rear edge of this lobe sits the Motor Cortex, which controls most types of skeletal movement.
  • Parietal Lobes: The part of the cerebral cortex that processes sensory information such as touch, temperature, and pain, and helps with spatial awareness and perception. It holds the Somatosensory Cortex, an area at the front of the parietal lobes that registers and processes body touch and movement sensations.
  • Occipital Lobes: The part of the cerebral cortex located at the back of the brain that is primarily responsible for processing visual information. It contains your primary visual cortex.
  • Temporal Lobes: The part of the cerebral cortex involved in processing auditory information, language comprehension, and memory. It houses your auditory cortex, letting you interpret sounds, tones, and speech nuances.
  • Association Areas: Areas of the cerebral cortex that are not involved in primary motor or sensory functions; rather, they are involved in higher mental functions like learning, remembering, thinking, and speaking. These regions dynamically piece together information across different lobes.

定 The Historic Case of Phineas Gage: In 1848, a railroad construction foreman named Phineas Gage survived a horrific accident when a blasting explosion blew a 3.5-foot iron tamping rod completely through his frontal lobe. While he miraculously survived, could still walk, and retained his memories, his personality transformed overnight. Formerly dependable, polite, and business-savvy, Gage became fitful, irreverent, and highly profane. This profound historical case study provided early, definitive proof of localization of function, demonstrating to early psychologists that the frontal lobes are explicitly responsible for personality, emotional regulation, and executive functioning.

Part 3: Hemispheric Specialization and Split-Brain Research

The brain operates under a unique system of shared and divided responsibilities between its two halves.

The Cross-Wired Mind

The human brain displays Contralateral Organization, meaning the left hemisphere controls and processes information from the right side of the body, while the right hemisphere manages the left side. The two halves exhibit Hemispheric Specialization, where certain cognitive tasks are handled more efficiently by one side over the other. In a typical brain, these halves coordinate constantly across the Corpus Callosum, a thick band of neural fibers that connects the left and right hemispheres of the brain, allowing communication between them.

When patients suffer from severe, unmanageable epilepsy, surgeons sometimes sever this connection as a treatment option. This procedure opened the door for classic Split Brain Research, which studies how the left and right hemispheres function independently and specialize in different tasks when they can no longer talk to each other.

The Left-Hemisphere Language Engine

Split-brain studies highlighted that language centers are heavily localized in the left hemisphere for most individuals:

  • Broca’s Area: A region of the left frontal lobe involved in speech production and language expression. Damage here leaves a person understanding speech perfectly but struggling to physically produce articulate words.
  • Wernicke’s Area: A region of the left temporal lobe involved in language comprehension. Damage here allows a person to speak fluently, but their sentences are a meaningless jumble of words, and they cannot understand what others are saying.
  • Aphasia: A general language disorder caused by damage to areas of the brain responsible for language, resulting in difficulties with speaking, understanding, reading, or writing.

💡 How Researchers Test Split-Brain Specialization: Because of contralateral organization, showing an image to a patient's far right visual field sends that data straight to the left hemisphere. Since language is based in the left hemisphere, the patient can easily say out loud what they see. However, if an image is shown to their left visual field, it travels to the right hemisphere. The patient will claim they saw nothing because the right hemisphere cannot speak, but their left hand (controlled by the right hemisphere) can easily point to or draw the object!

Part 4: Plasticity and Tools of Discovery

How do we know about the various brain structures? Sometimes it is from case studies of individuals with severe brain injuries (like Phineas Gage) but now modern neuroscience is able to utilize specialized tools to study its architecture and function in ways never possible before.

Adaptation: Brain Plasticity

The brain is not a rigid, unchangeable machine. It possesses Plasticity, the brain’s ability to change and reorganize itself by forming new neural connections in response to learning, experience, or injury. If a specific region is damaged, plasticity often allows surrounding tissues to rewire and take over those lost functions, a trait that is especially powerful during childhood development.

Neuroscience Methods: Mapping the Brain

Historically, scientists could only study the brain by analyzing a Lesion—an area of damaged or destroyed tissue in the body or brain—often studied after an injury or surgery to understand what functions were lost when that specific region was compromised. Today, researchers use a variety of non-invasive scanning technologies to observe the living brain:

  • EEG: Electroencephalogram; an amplified recording of the waves of electrical activity sweeping across the brain's surface. It uses electrode-studded caps to track rapid electrical changes, making it ideal for sleep and epilepsy studies.
  • CT Scan: A medical imaging technique that uses X-rays to create detailed cross-sectional images of the brain and other parts of the body. It provides a clear view of structural damage, like a tumor or a stroke, but does not show active brain function.
  • MRI: A brain imaging technique that uses magnetic fields and radio waves to produce highly detailed images of the brain’s structure and other body tissues. It offers much higher structural resolution than a CT scan.
  • PET Scan: A brain imaging technique that uses a radioactive glucose tracer to measure activity and energy use in different areas of the brain. It shows which parts of the brain are working hardest during a mental task by tracking metabolic demand.
  • fMRI: Functional MRI; a brain imaging technique that measures changes in blood flow to show which areas of the brain are active during specific mental tasks. By combining high-resolution structure with real-time functional tracking, it has become a cornerstone of modern cognitive neuroscience.

Common Misconceptions: Don't Trip Up!

⚠️ The "Left-Brained vs. Right-Brained" Myth: Pop psychology often claims that creative people are "right-brained" while logical thinkers are "left-brained." This is a complete misconception! While the hemispheres show specialization for certain tasks (like language on the left), healthy brains use both hemispheres together for almost every complex task, communicating through the corpus callosum. You are not one or the other; you use your entire brain.

⚠️ Broca’s vs. Wernicke’s Area: Students easily swap these language centers. Remember: Broca's area is for Babbling (speech production), while Wernicke's area is for What? (speech comprehension and understanding).
