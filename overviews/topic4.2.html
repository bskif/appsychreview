Topic 4.2: Attitude Formation and Attitude Change

The Big Picture: How We View the World and Ourselves

Have you ever wondered why it is so hard to change someone's mind, or why people jump to conclusions about others they have never met? Our attitudes—the feelings and beliefs that dictate how we respond to objects, people, and events—are shaped heavily by our environment and our own mental shortcuts. In this section, we explore how attitudes form, why they are so stubborn, and how underlying biases influence our behavior toward different social groups.

1. Stereotypes, Prejudice, and Discrimination

A stereotype is a generalized concept about a group. It is essentially a generalized belief or expectation about a group of people that assumes members of the group share the same characteristics, often oversimplifying reality. Why do our brains rely on them? Stereotypes can help reduce cognitive load—the amount of mental effort being used in working memory—when making decisions or judgments.

However, this mental shortcut comes at a severe cost. Stereotypes can be the cause and/or result of biased perceptions and experiences and are frequently the basis of prejudiced attitudes and discriminatory behaviors.

  • Prejudice: A preconceived negative attitude or belief about a group of people based on characteristics such as race, ethnicity, gender, or religion, often without sufficient evidence or personal experience.
  • Discrimination: Unjustified negative behavior or unequal treatment toward individuals based on their membership in a particular group, such as race, gender, religion, or ethnicity.

2. The Power of Implicit Attitudes

Not all of our attitudes are out in the open. Implicit attitudes are those that individuals hold but may be unaware of or may not acknowledge. These unconscious beliefs or feelings toward people, objects, or groups can influence behavior without conscious awareness. Research has focused on how implicit attitudes reflect negative evaluations of others, as demonstrated by several powerful cognitive phenomena:

  • Just-World Phenomenon: The tendency to believe that people get what they deserve and deserve what they get, leading to the assumption that good things happen to good people and bad things happen to bad people.
  • Out-Group Homogeneity Bias: The tendency to see members of an out-group as more similar to each other than they actually are, while viewing one’s own in-group as more diverse.
  • In-Group Bias: The tendency to favor and give preferential treatment to members of one’s own group over those in other groups.
  • Ethnocentrism: The belief that one’s own culture or group is superior to others, often leading to judging other cultures based on one’s own cultural standards.

3. Cognitive Friction: Belief Perseverance and Dissonance

Our brains deeply prefer consistency, and when that consistency is threatened by new evidence or conflicting actions, we react in predictable ways to resolve the tension.

Belief perseverance occurs when a belief persists even if evidence suggests it is not accurate. This is the tendency to maintain a belief even after the evidence supporting it has been discredited or contradicted. People experiencing belief perseverance may engage in confirmation bias (the tendency to seek out, interpret, and remember information that supports existing beliefs while ignoring or discounting contradictory evidence), thereby clinging to an attitude or belief regardless of the evidence for or against it.

But what happens when our internal thoughts conflict with our external actions? Cognitive dissonance refers to the mental discomfort that occurs when actions or attitudes are in conflict. It is the psychological discomfort that occurs when a person holds conflicting beliefs, attitudes, or behaviors. To resolve this, people are motivated to reduce the discomfort by changing either actions or attitudes to be more in line with each other.

4. The Power of Roles

Our attitudes are profoundly shaped by the social roles we step into. A role is a set of expectations about how people in a specific social position should behave. When we adopt a new role, we quickly adopt the attitudes that accompany it.

The most famous (and highly controversial) demonstration of this was Philip Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment. College students randomly assigned to be "guards" or "prisoners" quickly adopted the aggressive or submissive attitudes expected of those roles, taking the simulation so seriously that the experiment had to be shut down early. It revealed a striking truth: what we do, we gradually become.

The Stanford Prison Experiment and the Power of Roles. Conducted by Philip Zimbardo in 1971, this landmark study randomly assigned roles of guards and prisoners to college students in a simulated prison environment. The experiment demonstrated how powerfully assigned social roles and situational variables can compel individuals to conform to expected behaviors, often overriding internal personality traits. The rapid internalization of roles, sometimes leading to abusive or compliant behaviors, highlights critical concepts within AP Psychology's exploration of social roles (Topic 4.2).

5. Don't Trip Up! (Common Misconceptions)

⚠️ Prejudice vs. Discrimination: It is crucial to distinguish between thoughts and actions. Prejudice is an attitude (a thought or feeling), whereas discrimination is a behavior (an action). You can harbor prejudice without acting on it, but when you translate that prejudice into unequal treatment, it becomes discrimination.

⚠️ Confirmation Bias vs. Belief Perseverance: Confirmation bias happens during the active search for information (you selectively seek out sources that already agree with you). Belief perseverance happens after you are explicitly presented with definitive evidence that proves you wrong, but you still refuse to change your mind.
