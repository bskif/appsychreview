Unit 4: Social Psychology and Personality

Topic 4.2: Attitude Formation and Attitude Change

Last Updated: July 8, 2026

The Big Picture: Constructing Our Social World

Every day, we encounter a barrage of new people, ideas, and experiences. To navigate this complexity, we develop attitudes—learned evaluations or beliefs about people, objects, and ideas. Some of these attitudes are built carefully through logic, but many are formed implicitly or adopted to make quick sense of our environments. In this topic, we will explore how negative attitudes form, the unconscious biases that drive them, and the psychological mechanisms that make changing our minds so difficult.

1. Stereotypes, Prejudice, and Discrimination

Our brains process an overwhelming amount of information every second. To cope, we often categorize people into groups, but this mental shortcut can lead to harmful consequences.

  • Stereotypes: A stereotype is a generalized concept about a group[cite: 3]. Stereotypes can help reduce cognitive load when making decisions or judgments[cite: 3]. Example: Assuming that all older adults are bad with technology is a stereotype that saves a person the mental effort of evaluating each older individual's actual skills.
  • Prejudice: Stereotypes can be the cause and/or result of biased perceptions and experiences and are frequently the basis of prejudiced attitudes and discriminatory behaviors[cite: 3]. Prejudice is a preconceived negative attitude or belief about a group of people based on characteristics such as race, ethnicity, gender, or religion, often without sufficient evidence or personal experience. Example: Feeling resentful toward a new coworker simply because of the country they immigrated from.
  • Discrimination: This involves unjustified negative behavior or unequal treatment toward individuals based on their membership in a particular group, such as race, gender, religion, or ethnicity. Example: A hiring manager throwing away a highly qualified applicant's resume because of their gender.

⚠️ Don't Trip Up! It is easy to confuse prejudice and discrimination. Remember that prejudice is an attitude (a thought or feeling), whereas discrimination is an action (a behavior). You can have a prejudiced thought without acting on it, but acting on it becomes discrimination.

2. Implicit Attitudes and Social Biases

Not all attitudes are worn on our sleeves. Implicit attitudes are those that individuals hold but may be unaware of or may not acknowledge[cite: 3]. While we might claim to be completely impartial, these unconscious beliefs or feelings toward people, objects, or groups can influence behavior without conscious awareness. Research has focused on how implicit attitudes reflect negative evaluations of others, as demonstrated by the following concepts[cite: 3]:

  • In-Group Bias: The tendency to favor and give preferential treatment to members of one's own group over those in other groups. Example: A teacher giving extra leeway on a late assignment to a student who plays for the school's football team, while penalizing a student who does not.
  • Out-Group Homogeneity Bias: The tendency to see members of an out-group as more similar to each other than they actually are, while viewing one's own in-group as more diverse. Example: A teenager claiming that "all teachers are boring and strict," while believing that the students at the school have wonderfully unique and diverse personalities.
  • Ethnocentrism: The belief that one's own culture or group is superior to others, often leading to judging other cultures based on one's own cultural standards. Example: Assuming that a foreign culture's traditional food is "gross" simply because it differs from what you grew up eating.
  • Just-World Phenomenon: The tendency to believe that people get what they deserve and deserve what they get, leading to the assumption that good things happen to good people and bad things happen to bad people. Example: Hearing that someone lost their job and assuming they must have been a lazy employee, rather than considering that the company simply downsized.

3. Resisting Change: Belief Perseverance

Once we form an attitude, it can be incredibly stubborn. Belief perseverance occurs when a belief persists even if evidence suggests it is not accurate[cite: 3]. People experiencing belief perseverance may engage in confirmation bias, thereby clinging to an attitude or belief regardless of the evidence for or against it[cite: 3]. Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek out, interpret, and remember information that supports existing beliefs while ignoring or discounting contradictory evidence.

Example: A person firmly believes that left-handed people are more creative. They notice and remember every famous left-handed artist they encounter (confirmation bias) and completely ignore statistics showing no significant difference in creativity between left- and right-handed populations (belief perseverance).

4. Forcing Change: Cognitive Dissonance

So, how do attitudes ever change? One of the most powerful mechanisms is cognitive dissonance. Cognitive dissonance refers to the mental discomfort that occurs when actions or attitudes are in conflict[cite: 3]. People are motivated to reduce the discomfort by changing either actions or attitudes to be more in line with each other[cite: 3].

Example: A student considers themselves a highly honest person (attitude) but decides to cheat on a major exam (behavior). This creates psychological tension. To relieve this discomfort, they must either stop cheating in the future (changing behavior) or justify the action by telling themselves, "The test was unfair anyway, so cheating was justified" (changing attitude).
