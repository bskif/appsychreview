Unit 2: Cognition

Topic 2.1: Perception

Last Updated: June 30, 2026

The Big Picture: Can You Always Trust Your Senses?

In Unit 1, we learned how our biological hardware collects raw sensory data from the environment. But sensing the world is only half the battle. Our brains must actively filter, organize, and interpret that data to create a meaningful reality—a process known as perception. Because perception relies heavily on our memories, expectations, and biases, the reality we experience isn't just a perfect video recording of the world; it is a mental construction. Let's explore how our brains build that reality and why they sometimes get it wrong.

1. Making Sense of the World: Two Processing Speeds

Our brains combine incoming data with preexisting knowledge using two distinct pathways:

  • Bottom-Up Processing: Starting with the raw sensory input and building up to a complete perception. You use this when looking at something completely new and unfamiliar.
  • Top-Down Processing: Using your prior knowledge, expectations, and experience to interpret sensory information. This is why you can read a sentence even if some of the letters are jumbled or missing.

Your brain relies heavily on Schemas (mental frameworks organized by past experiences) to establish Context. Context and cultural expectations heavily filter how sensory input is interpreted in any given situation.

2. The Filters: Attention and Inattention

Millions of sensory inputs hit your receptors every second. Attention is the cognitive process of focusing awareness on specific stimuli while ignoring the rest.

  • Selective Attention: The ability to focus on one specific stimulus while filtering out distractions. A famous example is the Cocktail Party Effect—your ability to focus on a single conversation in a noisy room while still subconsciously monitoring the room for personally relevant information (like hearing someone say your name across the room).
  • Change Blindness: A fascinating perceptual failure where people completely fail to notice significant changes in a visual scene simply because their attention was focused elsewhere.

3. Gestalt Principles: The Whole is Greater Than the Parts

Early German psychologists noticed that human beings have a natural tendency to organize visual elements into groups or unified wholes. These are known as Gestalt principles:

  • Closure: The brain's tendency to fill in missing parts of a visual image to perceive it as a complete, solid shape.
  • Similarity: Grouping objects together that look similar in appearance (like shape, color, or size).
  • Proximity: (Often paired with similarity) Grouping objects together that are physically close to one another.
  • Figure and Ground: The organization of the visual field into objects (the figures) that stand out from their surroundings (the background).

4. Perceiving Depth and Motion

Your retina only receives a 2D image, yet you navigate a 3D world effortlessly. Your brain calculates depth using two categories of visual cues:

Binocular Depth Cues (Requires Two Eyes)

  • Retinal Disparity: Because your eyes are roughly 2.5 inches apart, they receive slightly different images of the world. The brain compares these two images; the greater the disparity (difference) between them, the closer the object must be.
  • Convergence: A neuromuscular cue where your eyes literally turn inward to focus on a nearby object. The more your eye muscles strain inward, the closer the brain calculates the object to be.

Monocular Depth Cues (Requires Only One Eye)

These cues give the illusion of depth on a flat surface, which is exactly how painters create realistic 3D art on a 2D canvas.

  • Relative Size: Assuming objects are similar in size, the one casting a smaller retinal image is perceived as farther away.
  • Relative Clarity: Clear, sharp objects seem closer, while hazy objects seem farther away.
  • Texture Gradient: Coarse, detailed textures appear closer; smoother, blurred textures appear distant.

Visual Constancy & Apparent Motion

Your brain also maintains Visual Perceptual Constancy, meaning it recognizes that objects aren't actually changing size, shape, or color even as the lighting or angle shifts. Finally, the brain can be tricked into seeing motion where none exists:

  • Stroboscopic Motion: The illusion of continuous movement created by flashing slightly different still images rapidly (how animation and movies work).
  • Autokinetic Effect: A perceptual illusion where a single stationary point of light in a pitch-black room appears to drift or move due to the lack of visual reference points.

5. Don't Trip Up! (Common Misconceptions)

⚠️ Bottom-Up vs. Top-Down: A great way to remember this is to think of assembling a jigsaw puzzle. Bottom-Up is trying to put the puzzle together without looking at the box cover (building piece by piece). Top-Down is looking at the box cover first, so you already have an expectation of what you are looking for before you place the pieces!

⚠️ Retinal Disparity vs. Convergence: Retinal disparity is about the visual image difference on the retina. Convergence is about the physical muscle feeling of your eyes turning inward. Both require two eyes, but they rely on different mechanisms.
